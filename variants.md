# One Sentence, One Journey — Candidate Stories & Tournament

Contest: `u-gudril-cool-one-sentence-one-journey-410686ca`
Gate: UTF-8 text, 1–300 chars, exactly one sentence, no title, no second sentence.
Judge brief (in priority order): (1) complete journey story in one sentence; (2) a turn —
something changes between first and last clause; (3) whole arc inside the sentence
(beginning, middle, arrival, not a list); (4) concrete detail (named thing, sound, smell),
not abstract feeling; (5) final clause lands with weight. The strongest leave the reader
somewhere they did not expect to be.

Research used (see builder-report.md): one-sentence arc structure
[Protagonist wants Goal, but Conflict → Resolution/Change]; six-word-story craft —
"residue, not synopsis", loaded concrete symbols, punchline/twist ending; every word
doing double duty.

## Candidates (all original, written in this session)

1. Mara rode the last night bus out of Lagos with her mother's brass key in her pocket,
   and when the driver dropped her at the village gate at dawn, the house she had fled
   ten years earlier was already lit, waiting, as if it had never let her go.

2. Old Yusuf sold his fishing boat to buy his grandson a train ticket to the city, then
   walked the empty shore every evening until the day a letter arrived saying the boy
   had bought the boat back.

3. The night train from Kano broke down in the desert, so the passengers shared one pot
   of tea under the stars, and by morning the strangers who had boarded as enemies were
   trading phone numbers like old friends.

4. He set out to scatter his father's ashes at the top of Mount Kilimanjaro, but at the
   summit the wind took the ashes back down the mountain, so he laughed, and followed
   them home.

5. Tunde ran from the village with nothing but a stolen bicycle and his brother's name on
   his tongue, and twenty years later he rode back on a bus full of medicine, the
   bicycle bell still ringing in his pocket.

6. She boarded the ferry to bury her husband at sea, but when the captain asked for his
   name, she gave her own instead, and sailed on to the island where they had first met.

7. The camel driver promised the tourists the oasis was one more dune away, and after
   the third dune he admitted he was lost, so they followed the smell of rain instead
   and found the water before dark.

8. When the bridge washed out, the schoolteacher carried her pupils across the river on
   her back one by one, and the last child she carried was the engineer who would
   rebuild the bridge thirty years later.

9. He walked the entire Camino with a stone in his shoe to remember his dead wife, and
   at the cathedral he took the stone out, set it on the altar, and walked home
   barefoot, lighter than he had felt in years.

10. The runaway bride caught the midnight train in her wedding dress, and by the time it
    reached the coast she had sold the dress to a fisherman's wife, bought a boat ticket
    with the coins, and never once looked back at the shore.

11. The boy who stowed away in a cargo ship to find his father spent the voyage hiding
    in a crate of mangoes, and when the ship docked he stepped out to find his father
    waiting on the pier, holding a crate of mangoes of his own.

12. She drove the ambulance through the flood to reach the hospital, and when the water
    took the road, she carried the patient the last mile herself, arriving on foot just
    as the baby took its first breath.

## Pairwise tournament (judged against the brief, criteria in order)

### Round 1
- A: #8 (bridge) vs #4 (ashes) — **#8 wins.** Both turn, but #8's final clause spans
  thirty years and closes the whole arc (the rescued becomes the rebuilder); #4's turn
  is a single beat and its ending, while charming, lands lighter.
- B: #6 (ferry) vs #9 (Camino) — **#6 wins.** The name-swap is a sharper, more
  surprising turn than the stone ritual, and "sailed on to the island where they had
  first met" lands with more weight than the slightly abstract "lighter than he had
  felt in years".
- C: #2 (boat) vs #5 (bicycle bell) — **#5 wins.** Both are full-circle returns, but
  the bell ringing in his pocket is a more specific, sensory concrete detail than the
  letter, and the medicine bus gives the return a clearer purpose.
- D: #10 (bride) vs #11 (mangoes) — **#10 wins.** The dress-to-coins-to-ticket chain
  gives a fuller beginning/middle/arrival; #11 is sweet but simpler, with less change
  between first and last clause.
- E: #1 (brass key) vs #12 (ambulance) — **#12 wins.** The road washing out forces a
  harder turn (carry the patient herself), and the baby's first breath is a heavier
  landing than the house "as if it had never let her go", which leans abstract.
- F: #3 (night train) vs #7 (camel driver) — **#7 wins.** "Followed the smell of
  rain" is concrete sensory detail; #3's "trading phone numbers like old friends" is
  a familiar, near-cliché image and its turn is the mildest of the field.

### Semi-finals
- SF1: #8 (bridge) vs #6 (ferry) — **#8 wins.** Both have strong turns, but #8's
  payoff closes the entire arc with a bigger surprise and a longer span; #6 leaves
  real ambiguity about what happens next, which weakens its completeness.
- SF2: #5 (bicycle bell) vs #10 (bride) — **#10 wins**, by a hair. Both are complete
  arcs with concrete objects; the bride's chain (dress → coins → ticket → never
  looking back) carries one more visible change of state.
- SF3: #12 (ambulance) vs #7 (camel driver) — **#12 wins.** Higher stakes, clearer
  turn, heavier final clause; #7's ending ("found the water before dark") resolves
  but does not surprise.

### Final (three-way)
- #8 (bridge) vs #10 (bride) vs #12 (ambulance) — **#8 WINS.**
  - Criterion 1: all three are complete one-sentence journeys; #8's is the most
    self-contained (no unexplained setup).
  - Criterion 2: #8's turn is the biggest — a child carried to safety becomes the
    adult who rebuilds the bridge; the reader ends thirty years from where they
    started, somewhere unexpected.
  - Criterion 3: clearest beginning (bridge out) / middle (carrying, one by one) /
    arrival (bridge rebuilt).
  - Criterion 4: concrete throughout — bridge, river, pupils on her back.
  - Criterion 5: "the engineer who would rebuild the bridge thirty years later"
    lands with the most weight; it reframes everything before it.
  - Runner-up: #10 (bride). Third: #12 (ambulance).

## WINNER (before revision)
> When the bridge washed out, the schoolteacher carried her pupils across the river on
> her back one by one, and the last child she carried was the engineer who would
> rebuild the bridge thirty years later.

## SECOND REVISION PASS (on the winner)
- "was the engineer who would rebuild the bridge" → "grew up to be the engineer who
  rebuilt it": "grew up" makes the thirty-year span explicit and earned; "rebuilt it"
  (past tense, done deed) lands harder than "would rebuild"; "it" trims a repeated
  "the bridge".
- Kept "one by one" (the middle of the arc, the slow labour that earns the ending)
  and "on her back" (the concrete physical image).
- Checked: exactly one sentence (single terminal period, at the end, no abbreviations),
  no title, no second sentence.

## FINAL WINNING SENTENCE
> When the bridge washed out, the schoolteacher carried her pupils across the river on
> her back one by one, and the last child she carried grew up to be the engineer who
> rebuilt it thirty years later.
